Phoenix in four parts

To keep the comparison simple, we view our own product in four parts:

Part One
Iranian competitors
Companies that sell the same kind of technology in Iran. For each one we ask: what feature does it have that we should also have, and how can we build it better?

Sokhan — a complete smart contact center

Website: sokhan.ai · Announced customers: banking, insurance, agriculture, transportation

Why it is similar to us: Like us, it has a smart Persian phone line (Rasa, no menu), analysis of all calls (Qualex) and a chatbot built on a language model (Lenabot). It is the only Iranian company that has three of our four parts together.

What they built

ProductFunction
Rasa — smart call routingInstead of a "for … press 1" menu, the caller says what they want freely, and the system sends them to the right place or answers itself. 24 hours a day, with transfer to an agent.
Qualex — call quality controlAll calls (not a sample) are turned into text and analyzed: script compliance, talk time of each side, silence, emotion (dissatisfaction, satisfaction, stress), transfers and dropped calls, speech rate, keywords. Automatic scoring with configurable criteria, real-time alerts to the supervisor, daily feedback to each agent, identification of each agent's training needs, and a dashboard with 20+ metrics.
Yarex — agent assistantDuring the call, it shows customer information from the CRM, suggests answers based on what the customer means, automates after-call work and writes a summary.
TikabotAnswers in messaging apps (Bale, Telegram, WhatsApp, Instagram) based on a knowledge tree, with referral to a human.
LenabotA chatbot built on a large language model for web and messaging apps.

How it works

According to them: Persian-specific speech-to-text, separating each speaker's voice and noise reduction, followed by text analysis. Connection to VoIP, the organization's PBX and the cloud. No further details have been published.

What we have today

  • Open conversation start with no menu (the same idea as Rasa) — Have
  • Analysis of all calls: topic, sentiment, summary, inappropriate words — Have
  • Agent scorecard with file export — Partial
  • Real-time alert for a specific call — Missing (trend alerts only)
  • Automatic periodic feedback to each person — Missing
  • Live agent assistant — Missing
  • Messaging apps — Partial (the Rubika tool exists but is off)

How to build it, and what inspiration we take

  1. Automatic periodic feedback: Adapt the "daily feedback to the agent" idea for HR: every week, send the manager of each unit a short summary of the most frequent topics in their unit (only if the number of people has reached the minimum threshold). Built from the existing analytics data. Easy
  2. Sensitive-call alert: If a topic such as harassment, a safety hazard or a threat comes up in a call, notify an authorized, restricted role — not the direct manager. Easy
  3. HR agent assistant: When a call is transferred to a human HR agent, the conversation summary and suggested answers from the knowledge base appear on the agent's screen. Hard (new user interface and PBX integration)
Bottom line: Sokhan is the most complete Iranian suite for contact centers. Our advantage should lie in HR domain knowledge, listening to the employee voice and protecting privacy; these are not seen in Sokhan's products.

Amerandish — smart operator and contact center monitoring

Website: amerandish.com · Products: Hooshtel, Farsava, Botava, Monitoring dashboard

Why it is similar to us: Hooshtel does the same job as our phone line (listen, transcribe, understand, answer, hand over to a human), and its monitoring dashboard does the same job as our call analytics.

What they built

  • Hooshtel: A smart phone operator on VoIP; it listens, transcribes, understands and answers like an operator; if it can't find an answer, it hands the call to a human.
  • Saipa and Iran Khodro project: Answering repetitive questions, listing the dealerships in each city, registering roadside-assistance requests and repair appointments, registering complaints with automatic speech-to-text, following up on registration renewals. Claim: about 80% of questions are similar and get automated.
  • Contact center monitoring dashboard: Turning the entire conversation into text and reporting by keyword, along with a detailed list of metrics (table below).

Metrics they measure

Quantitative metricsQualitative metrics
Call duration · operator talk time · caller talk time · silence duration · wait time · abandoned calls · number of turns · repeated questions (a sign of not understanding) · call transfer rate · speech rate · call volume per number Did the caller get the right answer? · topic classification · voice loudness · instruction compliance · full conversation transcript

What we have today

We have two-channel recording (each side's voice separately) and the timing of each sentence, so we can compute most of the quantitative metrics above without a new model. We do not have "Did they get an answer?".

How to build it, and what inspiration we take

  1. Quantitative metrics for each call: From the sentence timings of the two channels: talk time of each side, long silences, overlapping speech, number of turns. For the Employee Voice line this takes on a new meaning: very short calls or calls full of silence mean our question was badly designed. Easy
  2. "Did they get an answer?": At the end of a service desk call, a language model reads the transcript and determines whether the question was answered, referred or left unanswered. This is the basis for measuring "resolution without a human". Easy
  3. Voice complaint registration: The same Iran Khodro complaint-registration pattern, for "report a workplace problem". Medium
Bottom line: Technologically, Amerandish is the closest domestic company to us, but it has not announced any HR use case. Its list of metrics is a ready-made checklist for our dashboard.

Sitak — AI-based call quality control

Website: sitak.co

Why it is similar to us: Its processing chain (two-channel recording → text → summary → sensitive words → alert) is, step by step, the same chain as our call analytics.

How it works (according to their own guide)

  1. Two-channel audio recording (operator and customer separately).
  2. Sending to the AI system and converting speech to text.
  3. Analyzing the text and producing five outputs: a summary for the customer record; detection of sensitive words (complaint, threat, competitor name); measurement of satisfaction, tone, politeness and compliance with opening and closing phrases; discovery of costly processes; and a critical-call alert (tension, insults, request for a manager).

What we have today

We have two-channel recording, speech-to-text, summaries, inappropriate words for each side, and sentiment. We do not have a "customer-definable sensitive word list" or alerts for a single call.

How to build it

  1. Editable sensitive word list: An HR specialist defines a list (such as "آزار" ("harassment"), "تبعیض" ("discrimination"), "حادثه" ("accident")) and calls containing these topics get tagged. Ideally the language model detects the "topic", not just the word itself. Easy
  2. Critical-call alert with the same rule as above, sent only to the authorized role. Easy

Foreign sources of inspiration
These companies are not our competitors. From their blogs and documentation we learn exactly how they built their key features. Ordered by the four parts of Phoenix.

Listening to the employee voice

Perceptyx — conversation instead of survey

Why it is similar to us: Exactly our pilot idea, only with chat instead of phone: the employee talks, the assistant asks follow-up questions, comments are analyzed and the manager asks questions of the comments.

What they built: Instead of filling in a form, the employee says what they think in a chat-like conversation. The assistant starts the conversation at key moments (such as the end of the probation period) and asks follow-up questions based on what that person said.

How they analyze comments (from their own technical paper, Feb 2026):

TaskMethod
Finding the topicThree methods together: a model trained on labeled examples, a keyword list, and a method that discovers new topics on its own.
Speaker intentEach comment falls into one of these categories: praise, wants and preferences, suggestion ("should…"), needs and concerns.
SentimentThree levels: positive, neutral, negative.
EmotionSix states: anger, fear, interest, joy, sadness, surprise.
PrivacyBefore any comment is sent to an external language model, people's names are masked using a name-recognition model. Customer data is not used to train the model. Every AI feature is labeled and the customer can turn it off.

Sentiment model: 1,000 real comments were labeled by four organizational psychologists following a shared guideline; these four people themselves disagreed in 7% of cases (meaning 100% accuracy is not possible). A small fine-tuned model reached 89.4% accuracy, almost equal to a large model with a precise prompt (89.3%) but cheaper and faster. "Mixed" comments ("My manager is good but the workload is high") were reviewed separately.

What we have today: We have topic and subtopic, sentiment, emotion and the "suggestions" column. We do not have new-topic discovery, name masking or an accuracy benchmark.

How to build it:

  1. Mask names and numbers before storage and analysis: rules for numbers (phone, personnel code) + a language model or name-recognition model for names. Easy
  2. An "intent" column next to sentiment; just one new prompt for the language model that already does the analysis. Easy
  3. New-topic discovery: comments that don't fit any existing topic are collected, grouped by semantic similarity, and each growing group is suggested to a specialist ("New topic: night-shift commuter shuttle"). Medium
  4. Persian sentiment benchmark: 1,000 anonymized excerpts from pilot calls, three labelers, one guideline. Easy

Qualtrics — smart follow-up questions mid-survey

Why it is similar to us: The same thing agreement row CON-04 asks of us: when an answer is vague, one targeted follow-up question.

How it works: When an employee answers an open question, the AI reads the answer and, if it is general or vague, asks a targeted follow-up question about that same point. In their experiments about 40% of respondents expanded their answers. Another important finding of theirs: only 48% of employees say they have seen the impact of their feedback; and wherever the organization listened more, engagement nearly doubled.

What we have today: The "follow-up question" branch exists in the conversation brain and works. There is no cap on the number of follow-up questions and no reporting of results back to employees.

How to build it: (1) A cap and rule for follow-up questions for each question Easy; (2) "We heard, we acted": after each cycle, a voice message or SMS to the same unit about what was done Easy.

Listen Labs — AI interviews at scale

Why it is similar to us: One assistant talks with hundreds of people at the same time and summarizes the results itself — the same thing our Employee Voice line does.

How it works (from their documentation):

  1. The researcher writes the goal in plain language; the system builds the interview guide (list of questions) and the screening questions.
  2. For each question a follow-up depth is chosen: none, only when the answer is short, once, or two to three times.
  3. For each question you can write a "probing instruction": exactly which aspect to look for.
  4. The system estimates the approximate interview length so it doesn't run long.
  5. Hundreds of interviews run at the same time; automated analysis delivers findings, themes and a ready report in less than a day.

How to build it — a "conversation plan" for each line: each line (pulse, exit interview, new-hire follow-up) has a list of questions, with follow-up depth and a probing instruction set for each question; the conversation brain proceeds from that plan. It also solves the gap noted in the pilot agreement (the follow-up question cap). Medium

Culture Amp — beware of "passive listening"

They consider passive analysis of employees' messages and emails "hype": only 24% of HR professionals recommend their organization's listening approach, and their own experiment on work messages showed that some groups (by region, gender, tenure) are less visible. Takeaway for us: voluntary, informed channels only.

Call analysis and evaluation

Observe.AI, Level AI and Calabrio — automated evaluation of all calls

Why it is similar to us: They automatically turn every call into text and a score — the same job as our call analytics — and give evidence and a confidence level for each result (agreement row AI-14).

What they built: Instead of a quality specialist listening to one or two of every hundred calls, the system scores all calls against an evaluation form and, for each answer, provides evidence (the relevant part of the conversation and its timestamp) and a reason. Claim: evaluation five times faster.

Rules for writing an evaluation form (from Calabrio's guide):

  1. Phrase the question as "Did the agent …?" and ask about observable behavior.
  2. Most questions should be yes/no; they give the most accurate results.
  3. Each question should measure only one behavior.
  4. For each question, write a description of exactly what to look for; for each option, a precise condition and a sample conversation.
  5. Scoring: yes = 100, no = 0, "not applicable" = excluded from the calculation. At most a five-point scale with equal intervals (100, 75, 50, 25, 0).
  6. "Not applicable" is when that behavior made no sense in that call (for example, the call was transferred).

Level AI says its system doesn't need to be trained on a list of phrases; it understands the intent of each question and gives evidence and reasoning for each answer. Observe.AI shows the score for each section of the form and an "auto-fail" for serious errors.

What we have today: An agent scorecard and analytic tags for each call. We do not have a "definable evaluation form" engine.

How to build it — an evaluation form engine:

  1. The specialist builds a form: question, description, options with conditions and examples.
  2. For each call and each question, the language model reads the transcript and returns: the answer, the evidence sentence, its timestamp in the call, and a short reason.
  3. The score is calculated with the rule above; calls the model is not confident about are flagged for human review.
  4. The specialist can correct any answer, and these corrections are collected to measure accuracy.
Dual use for us: The same engine measures (a) the quality of our own phone assistant ("Did the assistant ask an appropriate follow-up question?", "Was the call ending clear?") and (b) the quality of the HR service desk agents. Medium

Employee assistant and service desk

Leena AI — voice and text HR assistant

Why it is similar to us: A voice assistant that answers from an HR knowledge base and hands over to a human when it can't — the same as our phone line, conversation brain and knowledge base.

What they built: A ready-made "AI colleague" for HR (onboarding, leave, payroll questions, benefits), voice-enabled and available 24 hours a day in more than 100 languages, with ready connectors to 200+ systems and a tool that defines processes in plain sentences. Claim: 70%+ of requests are resolved without a human, set up in 45 days.

Lessons from a rollout to 51,000 employees of a hospital chain:

  1. The first year was spent on organizing content, security and internal alignment; internal alignment was the hardest part.
  2. First tasks: leave balance and leave requests, policy questions, process guides, filling in forms.
  3. The most effective way to win users: introducing the assistant at the new-hire orientation session; and games and in-person demos instead of announcements.
  4. Metrics: first use, repeat use, satisfaction with answer accuracy.
  5. "Getting things done" (not just answering) was essential.

HiBob and Perk — the right order for building a service desk

Perk "did not start with AI". The order: (1) all requests were gathered in a single portal; (2) the knowledge base was built from real questions; (3) repetitive tasks were automated; (4) AI was added last. Result: 60% of requests handled without a human, 40% reduction in first response time.

Moveworks — an assistant that doesn't guess

Why it is similar to us: An employee assistant with the same pattern as ours: understanding intent, asking when ambiguous, tools, referral to a human.

The problem: When a language model needs a case number or code, it makes up something that "looks right". Their solution:

  1. Every required value (leave type, date, code) is a defined "slot".
  2. The options are read from the system of record, not from the model's memory; the model only chooses among them.
  3. The selected value is locked and the rest of the conversation uses it.
  4. It only asks when there is ambiguity; for each slot it is set to "always ask", "infer if known" or "always infer".

The right definition of "resolution without a human": events are logged separately: intent understood, resolved, task done automatically, referred to a human, abandoned and later followed up through another channel. If the user got tired and left, it is not counted as "resolved".

How to build it: for personal service desk tasks, upgrade the current follow-up question branch to "slot + read from system of record + verbal confirmation" ("مرخصی استحقاقی، شنبه ۱۲ مهر؛ درست است؟" ("Annual leave, Saturday 12 Mehr [4 Oct 2026]; is that right?")) Medium. Counting "resolution without a human": if the same number calls again within 72 hours, it is unresolved Easy.

UKG and SAP — voice for frontline employees

UKG launched its voice assistant with only 12 tasks: "What's my shift schedule this week?", "Where am I working today?", "Is there a problem with my timecard?", "Do I have enough time off for next week?", changing the payroll account. SAP built its assistant's voice on LiveKit — the same infrastructure our phone line runs on. Inspiration: the first phase of our voice service desk should also have 10 to 12 high-frequency tasks, each with its own test.

Paradox — calls and reminders

From 25 million interview appointments: timely reminders and easy rescheduling cut no-shows by 40%; 93% of reschedules were initiated by the organization itself, not the candidate. Inspiration for our outbound calls (exit interviews, new-hire follow-up): reminder SMS + "to reschedule, call this same number" + easy rescheduling on the HR side. Medium

Ask the data

Rippling — an answer that shows its work

Why it is similar to us: A question in everyday language → building a query over the data → answer and report; the same job as our ask-the-data assistant.
  1. A main assistant receives the question and passes it to one of 5 to 7 specialist assistants: reading data, searching the text of policies, or performing an action.
  2. The assistant sees only the data that the user themselves is allowed to see.
  3. The answer is shown together with the underlying query and report so it can be checked.
  4. Any change is first queued for approval and goes through the organization's own approval path.
  5. Testing in four layers: on every code change, after merge (300 to 400 questions), about 10 critical scenarios that block a release, and daily monitoring.

What we have today: The ask-the-data assistant (AQA) builds queries and is the only part of ours with automated tests. How to build it: show the query and row count next to each chart; pass the user's role into the query; anonymized views only for unit managers. Easy

Lattice — a report where every sentence has evidence

Why it is similar to us: It builds summaries from conversations and feedback; the same thing our per-call summary and monthly report do (row AI-03: fact-based summary).

It builds a performance review draft from real notes and feedback; every sentence links to its specific source; if data is thin it says "not enough information" instead of a generic sentence; the assistant doesn't score or submit — only a human does. Result: 47% faster. Inspiration: each topic in our monthly report should cite a few anonymized quotes and call numbers; below the minimum group size it should say "not enough data". Easy

Summary: what the complete product looks like

  1. Version 1 means trust and accuracy: most of the agreement's gaps are about privacy and measurement, not new AI. Every successful company in this space (Perceptyx, Porsline, Leena) has these foundations.
  2. A conversation that beats a form: the follow-up question cap and rule and the summary confirmation set our line apart from text surveys and IVR menus.
  3. From listening to action: sensitive-topic alerts in version 1, and manager summaries and "We heard, we acted" in version 2.
  4. A second product on the same engine: call evaluation, a voice service desk and outbound calls in version 2.
The key point: Sokhan and Amerandish have smart contact centers but no HR; Chargoon and Porsline have HR but no voice. Phoenix sits where these two meet: Persian telephony + smart conversation + listening to the employee voice + privacy protection + on-premises deployment. Version 1 makes this combination trustworthy for MCI; later versions complete it.
